Economics

Streaming vs. control

These tools will let you recover your music library from corporate streaming services. Submit your library and playlists, download the archive, and cancel your streaming subscriptions forever. Spend that money on live concerts, donating directly to artists, or buying real physical media that you don't have to rent forever.

The same math scales with bigger plans: the family plan ($19.99/month) comes to about $2,399 over ten years — that could be 160–480 albums, or a few concert tickets every year stacked with CD hauls. Albums typically run $5–15 at a record store, thrift shop, or online — and once you own them, they cost nothing more, ever.

Where the money goes

Streaming economics explain why so many artists are unhappy. Spotify pays artists on average about $0.0035 per stream — roughly $3,500 per million plays, before distributor fees. The UK government's 2020–21 inquiry into music streaming found a typical stream works out like this:

~$0.011 per stream total

  • Platform ~$0.004 (~33%)
  • Label ~$0.006 (~50%)
  • Publishing ~$0.002 (~17%)
  • Artist: a fraction of a cent

Record labels take the majority of every stream; artists are paid last, under contracts the labels wrote. A CD that sold for $13 was worth around 3,000 streams to the artist — streams they'd have to earn over and over again, for as long as anyone listens.

Buy it once — or pay for it every year

In 2005 the 30GB iPod video cost $299 and held roughly 500 hours of music — about 20 full days of continuous audio. The 60GB model cost $399 and held around 1,000 hours, over 40 days. The original 2001 iPod held just 5GB — "1,000 songs in your pocket" — for $399.

The average listener today streams about 20.7 hours of music a week (IFPI, 2023) — roughly 45 full days of continuous listening per year. That is a full year of listening, and it all fit on one 60GB iPod two decades ago. The hardware has been ready for a very long time; the business model wasn't.

A dedicated player: pay once, control it forever

Want to cut streaming out of your life completely? A dedicated audio player is the honest hardware answer. The Surfans F20 costs about $125, ships with 64GB of SD storage, and runs Rockbox, the open-source firmware — it plays only DRM-free files (FLAC, OGG, Opus, MP3, and more), has a headphone jack, and a line-out for your AUX input. No accounts, no app store, no phone required.

The included 64GB card alone holds roughly a year's worth of music for the average listener — and the player costs about $125, less than a single year of Spotify.

All of it under your control, all of it free of Digital Restrictions Management, none of it rented.

The player with its included card comes to about $125 — less than a single year of Spotify. Add the 512GB card and the whole setup is about $185, still less than two years of a subscription. Keep it for five years and it has paid for itself many times over, and you still control every file.

Artists who said it out loud

  • Taylor Swift pulled her entire catalog from Spotify in 2014. Her label said it had received under $500,000 for a year of her domestic streams — Spotify said the global total was $2 million. A year later she forced Apple to abandon its unpaid free-trial period by withholding her album 1989.
  • Thom Yorke of Radiohead pulled his side-project catalog from Spotify in 2013, calling the service 'the last desperate fart of a dying corpse'.
  • David Byrne pulled his catalog from Spotify and called streaming "a disaster for today's artists," noting the pittance the platforms pay.
  • Prince removed his catalog from Spotify and Apple Music in 2015, saying "Spotify wasn't paying, so you gotta shut it down."
  • Adele withheld her album 25 from all streaming services for seven months — it sold 3.38 million copies in its first week in the US alone.
  • Zoë Keating's published statements show the reality: 6,926 streams earned her about $32 in her first month on Spotify — fractions of a cent per stream.

Did algorithms really help you discover more?

So did algorithms make music discovery better, or did people just forget what it was like to hear something through a friend—a mixtape, a burned CD, “you have to listen to this”?

As algorithm dependence increases, people aren't just discovering more through the feed—they're relying less on the people they used to turn to for recommendations. Where recommendations used to come from human friends, communities, and public discussions, now streaming corporations are becoming the arbiter of recommendations, and they want you to stay dependent on them.

And there's the tradeoff: the algorithm can surface more songs, but it can't replace the human connection—someone taking the time to share a track because it means something to them. Streaming services aren't just trying to help you find music; they're trying to keep you in their app, coming back, and continuously consuming—so the easiest “recommendation” becomes the one that keeps you hooked, not the one that pulls you toward real conversations with real people.

And to be clear: these tools only grab the files from streaming platforms. What you do with them—keep them, share them, pass them on—is still up to you.

About These Tools

Library download: Imports can be downloaded from the library once the import is completed.

OPUS format: These tools convert audio to OPUS because it is far superior to other formats (better quality at the same bitrate, open/free codec). Learn more about OPUS superiority or read a plainer explanation. OPUS is chosen over lossless formats like FLAC because these tools are built for the average listener — OPUS at 160kbps takes over 10 times less space than the original, and even trained listeners have trouble telling the difference. FLAC support may be added later as storage gets cheaper.

Purpose: These tools exist to grow the music library, but they also let users recover their music collections and playlists from streaming services — taking back what the streaming corporations took from them — and go back to having control over their own media library.
